KLEAR / Glossary

Glossary

What the words KLEAR uses about its numbers mean. Each figure on the site also has a “What this means” link that explains that figure.

How sure a number is

Estimate

A number worked out from a sample or a model, not a full count. A survey asks some homes, not all of them. So the true number is likely close to the estimate, but not exactly the same.

Margin of error (±)

How far a survey estimate could be from the true number. KLEAR shows the Census Bureau’s 90 percent margin of error.

Say an estimate is 1,000 ± 100. Then we can be 90 percent sure the true number is between 900 and 1,100.

Within the margin of error

Two figures are this close when the gap between them is smaller than their margins of error. The gap could be due to chance, so KLEAR calls them about the same. It does not say one is higher.

Range

The low and high ends KLEAR gives for a figure it estimates for a district, such as “about 4,200 (range 3,100 to 5,600).” In most cases the true figure is inside the range. KLEAR gives a range for every apportioned or modeled estimate.

CV (coefficient of variation)

The margin of error compared with the size of the estimate. A high CV means the estimate is shaky.

KLEAR calls an estimate unreliable when its CV is over 30 percent. That is when its margin of error is about half the estimate or more. A larger area, such as the county, gives a steadier figure.

Median

The middle value: half are above it and half are below. For income, the median is often more useful than the average, since a few very high incomes do not pull it up.

Universe (who is counted)

The group a figure counts, or takes a percent of, such as all households or people 65 and over. Each figure’s “What this means” link shows it as “Who is counted.”

Where the numbers come from

ACS 5-year

The Census Bureau’s American Community Survey asks a sample of homes about their lives each year. The 5-year data pool five years of answers, such as 2020 to 2024, so that small areas like districts have enough of them.

A 5-year figure describes that whole time, not one year.

Vintage

The years or date a figure describes, such as “2020-2024 ACS 5-year” or “tax year 2023.”

A “current list” is a list of places, such as health centers, that KLEAR downloads fresh each time it builds its data.

Apportioned estimate

A figure KLEAR moves to a district from ZIP codes or census tracts, since their lines do not follow district lines. Each area’s count is split by where its people lived in the 2020 Census. KLEAR shows these with a range.

Modeled estimate

A figure worked out by a model, not counted or asked about in that place. CDC PLACES uses national survey answers and local facts, such as age and income, to estimate needs like food insecurity for each census tract. KLEAR shows these with a range.

Census tract

A small area the Census Bureau uses to report data, most often home to 2,500 to 8,000 people.

Income and programs

Poverty line (poverty level)

The income line the Census Bureau uses to count people in poverty. It depends on the size of the family and the ages of its members. It is the same in every state.

“200% of poverty” means an income below twice that line.

Poverty guideline

A simpler poverty line that the federal government uses to decide who can get some programs. Hawaiʻi has its own guidelines. They are higher than the mainland’s because it costs more to live here.

Census figures use the national poverty line, which is lower. So counts near a program’s limit may run low.

Area median income (AMI)

The middle family income in an area, set by HUD each year. Half of families earn more and half earn less. Housing programs set income limits as a share of it, such as 80 percent. HUD’s CHAS data calls it HAMFI.

Household and householder

A household is everyone who lives in one home, related or not. The householder is the person whose name the home is owned or rented in.

Kūpuna and keiki

Hawaiian words KLEAR uses for older people and children. Here, kūpuna means people 65 and over and keiki means children under 18, unless a label gives another age.

Race “alone” and “with other races”

On Census forms, people can choose one race or more. “Alone” counts people who chose only that race. “Alone or with other races” counts everyone who chose it, so one person can be counted in more than one group.

Work and school

Labor force

People 16 and over who have a job or are looking for one. People who are retired, in school or at home and not looking for work are not in it.

Unemployment rate

The share of the labor force that has no job and is looking for one. People who are not looking for work do not count.

Industry and occupation

Industry is the kind of business a person works for, such as a hotel or a school. Occupation is the kind of work the person does there, such as cook or teacher.

The Census Bureau counts workers where they live. So a district’s figures show the jobs of the people who live there, wherever the job is.

Educational attainment

The most schooling a person has finished. KLEAR shows it for adults 25 and over, such as the share with a high school diploma or a four-year college degree.
